What Is Insulation Made Of

Mostly air, held still by something else.

November 25, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

What is insulation made of, in the house you are standing in? Usually glass, rock, paper or plastic foam. In every one of them, the part doing the work is the air or gas the material holds still.

That sounds like a trick answer, and it is the most useful thing to know before you look in your attic. The material decides how well the air stays put, whether it minds getting wet, whether it burns, and what it does to your budget. Here is what each one actually is, how to tell which one you have, and when the cheap one is the right one.

The part that insulates is the air

Ask what is insulation made of and the honest first answer is air. Still air is a poor conductor of heat. Moving air carries heat away fast, which is why a breeze feels colder than the thermometer says. Insulation is a way of trapping a lot of air in tiny pockets so it can't move.

Fibre insulation does it with a tangle of fine strands. Foam does it with millions of small cells. That's why the same things ruin all of them: crush a batt and you squeeze the air out, leave a gap and the air goes round it. Our batt insulation page puts it plainly. Miss the fitting details and a nominal R-22 wall can perform closer to a fraction of it.

It's also why air leaks matter as much as the material. R-value only describes heat moving through the insulation. Warm air escaping through gaps around it takes heat with it, and in most Ontario houses that's the larger loss.

Fibreglass: spun glass

Fibreglass is glass. Sand and recycled glass are melted and spun into fine fibres, then held together with a binder. The pink or yellow colour is a dye and a brand, not a different product. It's the material most people picture, and the one that itches if you handle it bare-armed.

It comes two ways. Batts are pre-cut blankets friction-fit between studs, joists and rafters, the value choice for open, standard framing. Blown fibreglass is loose fibre. In an attic it's blown to an even depth. In a wall, the blown-in wall system we install (BIBS) staples netting across the open studs and blows fibre in behind it until the cavity is packed. That reaches up to R-24 in a 2x6 wall and R-14 in a 2x4, because it fills around the wiring and boxes a batt has to be cut around. Blown to density behind netting, it does not settle, and the fibre doesn't absorb moisture.

Mineral wool: melted rock

Mineral wool, often called rock wool, is made the same way from different stuff. Rock and slag are melted and spun into fibre. The result is heavier and denser than fibreglass, usually grey-green or brown.

That density buys three things. It holds its shape in a cavity without fasteners, it's non-combustible, and it's noticeably better at stopping sound. It also costs more. On our batt page we say we'll price either and tell you honestly where mineral wool is worth paying for. Party walls, mechanical rooms, home offices and floors between storeys are the usual places. A standard exterior wall usually isn't one.

Cellulose: recycled paper

Cellulose is shredded paper, mostly recycled newsprint, treated with borate chemicals so it resists fire, mould and insects. It's blown loose into attics or packed into closed walls. In an attic it looks like grey, fluffy shredded paper, and you can sometimes see bits of print in it.

It turns up in a lot of attics. If you're weighing it against blown fibreglass for a top-up, our post on cellulose vs fiberglass blown-in insulation goes through the trade-offs. The blown-in wall system we install is fibreglass.

Spray foam: two liquids that react on site

Spray foam is the one product made in your house rather than in a factory. Two liquid components, an isocyanate and a polyol resin, are mixed at the spray gun. They react and expand within seconds to many times their volume, then cure into a rigid plastic bonded to whatever they hit.

Closed-cell foam keeps gas sealed inside each cell, which is why it insulates better per inch than anything else we install, roughly R-6. The same layer acts as an air barrier and a vapour retarder. The foam we spray is Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA, a 2 lb closed-cell foam that uses soya-based renewable content in place of part of the petroleum-based polyol. It's made of recycled plastics, vegetable oil and soya, and has zero ozone-depleting substances.

Because the chemistry happens on site, so do the precautions. The space is ventilated, the crew wears supplied-air respirators, and you stay out for the manufacturer's re-entry period, which we give you for your job before we start. Cured foam is an inert plastic. Foam that's mixed off-ratio by bad equipment or an untrained installer is a real problem and very hard to fix afterwards. That's the reason the CAN/ULC-S705.2 installation standard exists, and why our applicators are certified to it.

Rigid foam board: polystyrene and polyiso

Foam also comes as factory-made boards, mostly used on basement walls, under slabs and on the outside of sheathing. There are three common kinds:

  • Expanded polystyrene (EPS): white beads fused into a sheet, the same family as a foam cooler.
  • Extruded polystyrene (XPS): a smooth, dense board, usually coloured by the manufacturer, that handles contact with damp soil well.
  • Polyisocyanurate (polyiso): a foam core between foil facers. Our post on polyiso foam board covers where it works and where cold weather costs it R-value.

All three are plastic foam with gas trapped in the cells. Read the RSI or R-value printed on the board, not the thickness, because the same thickness of each performs differently.

What is insulation made of in an older house?

Older houses are where the answer gets interesting, and where it matters most to know before anybody starts pulling material out.

Vermiculite

Vermiculite is a mined mineral that puffs up when heated into light, pebbly granules, grey-brown to gold and a bit shiny. It was poured loose between attic joists for decades. Older vermiculite may contain asbestos, and it has to be tested before anything disturbs it. If what you see looks like gravel or cat litter, leave it alone. Don't vacuum it, don't dig through it, and don't store boxes up there. Our insulation removal page and the post on vermiculite and attic insulation removal cover what happens next.

Shavings, sawdust and nothing at all

Houses old enough have wood shavings, sawdust or paper in places. Plenty of older walls have nothing. Balloon-framed houses often relied on the air gap in a double wall, which was the insulation of the day. Nothing in that gap stops the air moving, so those walls are cold to the touch in February.

What is insulation made of in walls you can't see?

Most people don't know what's in their exterior walls, and you don't have to open them to find out. With the power off at the panel, take the cover off an outlet on an outside wall and look at the edge of the box with a flashlight. You'll often see fibre, paper or nothing. If it's empty, those walls can be filled through 2-inch holes, usually drilled from inside, with the siding left untouched. That's the drill-and-fill work on our blown-in insulation page.

What is insulation made of in your attic? How to tell

Look from the hatch first. Wear a mask and gloves, keep your weight on the joists, and don't stir anything up.

  • Pink, yellow or white fluff, or blankets between the joists: fibreglass.
  • Grey fluff that looks like shredded paper: cellulose.
  • Dense grey-green or brown woolly clumps or batts: mineral wool.
  • Light, shiny pebbles: possibly vermiculite. Stop and get it tested.
  • A hard, sprayed-on surface on the roof deck or rim: spray foam.

Then check the depth. Settled insulation can be at half its original depth, and current attic targets are far deeper than what most homes built before the 1990s actually have. While you're up there, look for the things that matter more than the material: dark stains, frost on the nails in winter, droppings, or a bathroom fan venting into the attic. Wet, mouse-infested or flattened insulation has lost most of its value. Adding more on top of it hides the problem, it doesn't fix it.

So what is insulation made of, and does it matter which?

Less than the way it's installed. A well-fitted batt beats a badly fitted anything. Match the material to the problem:

  • Batts where the framing is open, clean and standard and budget matters most. They're the lowest cost per square foot.
  • Blown fibreglass where you want the cavity full around the wiring, better sound control, or insulation in walls that are already closed.
  • Spray foam where you need an air and vapour barrier, high R-value in a shallow space, or a seal on an awkward surface: rim joists, crawl spaces, cathedral ceilings.

Most houses we quote get a combination. We routinely foam the rim joists and problem areas and batt the rest, because that's the best result for the money. Foam is our most-requested service, and it still isn't the answer everywhere. If a cheaper product will do the same job in your house, we'll tell you.

The fastest way to know what's in yours is to let someone look. We get into the attic, basement or crawl space to see what is actually there, because we would rather look at your house than quote you a number from a brochure. You get a written, itemized quote with the reasoning behind each recommendation. Start with the quote request.

What Is Insulation Made Of — questions homeowners ask

What is insulation made of?

Most home insulation is glass fibre, rock wool, recycled paper or plastic foam. Fibreglass is melted sand and recycled glass, mineral wool is melted rock and slag, cellulose is shredded paper treated with borates, and spray foam and foam boards are plastic. In each one the insulating is done by still air or gas trapped in the material.

What is the pink insulation made of?

Glass. Pink and yellow insulation is fibreglass: sand and recycled glass melted, spun into fibre and held with a binder. The colour is a dye that identifies the manufacturer, not a different material.

What is spray foam insulation made of?

Two liquids, an isocyanate and a polyol resin, mixed at the spray gun so they react and expand into a rigid foam. The closed-cell foam we install, Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA, replaces part of the petroleum-based polyol with soya-based renewable content.

Is old insulation in my attic dangerous?

Most of it isn't, but vermiculite can be. It looks like light, shiny grey-brown pebbles, and older vermiculite may contain asbestos. Leave it undisturbed and have it tested before any work in the attic. Insulation contaminated by mice, bats or a roof leak should also come out rather than be covered over.

Which insulation material is best?

The one that suits the space, installed properly. Batts are the cheapest per square foot for open, standard framing; blown fibreglass fills cavities completely and controls sound; closed-cell spray foam gives the most R-value per inch and doubles as an air and vapour barrier. Most houses use more than one.

Does the insulation material matter more than the installation?

No. Compressed batts, gaps at the edges and insulation crushed behind wiring can leave a wall performing well below its rating, whatever it's made of. Air leaks around the insulation matter too, so air sealing comes first.
