Insulate the walls and the ground rules change with them.

November 23, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

A crawl space vapor barrier in Ontario is not one spec. Part 9 has two ground cover articles, one for unheated crawl spaces and one for heated ones, and the gap between them is 0.10 mm poly with plain laps against 0.15 mm CGSB poly that is sealed, weighted and tied into the air barrier.

Which one applies isn't something you pick when you buy the roll. It follows from where the insulation goes. Put it on the foundation walls, which is where it usually belongs, and you have built a heated crawl space whether anyone called it that or not. The ground cover has to meet the stricter article, and a lot of the ones that fail inspection were laid to the easier one.

Heated or unheated: the call that sets the crawl space vapor barrier

The two articles sit side by side in Subsection 9.18.6 of the Ontario Building Code. Read them together and the difference is obvious.

Unheated: Article 9.18.6.1

An unheated crawl space needs a ground cover of at least 0.10 mm polyethylene, Type S roll roofing, 50 mm of asphalt or 100 mm of 15 MPa concrete. Sheet joints are lapped at least 100 mm and weighted down. Nothing in the article asks you to seal the laps or seal the edge to the wall. The space itself has to be ventilated, and under Article 9.18.3.1 natural ventilation means at least 0.1 m² of unobstructed vent area per 50 m² of floor, spread across opposite sides of the building and screened against snow, rain and insects.

Heated crawl space vapor barrier: Article 9.18.6.2

A heated crawl space needs at least 0.15 mm polyethylene conforming to CAN/CGSB-51.34-M, installed as part of the air barrier system under Subsection 9.25.3. That's a 6 mil CGSB sheet, and the marking on the roll matters more than the thickness, as our post on 6 mil poly vapour barrier covers. The joints are lapped at least 300 mm and both sealed and weighted down. The alternative is a concrete skim coat of at least 50 mm over the poly. Either way, the perimeter of the ground cover is sealed to the foundation wall.

So the heated crawl space vapor barrier is doing a second job. In an unheated space the sheet keeps ground moisture down. In a heated one it is a piece of the building's air barrier, held to the same continuity as the poly behind the drywall upstairs.

A note for the binder: that article text is quoted from the 2012 OBC as published. The 2024 edition has been in force since January 2025, so check the wording and numbering against the current code before you set a detail on paper.

You may already be heated without meaning to be

The national model code that Part 9 is built on treats a crawl space as heated in three situations: it's used as a warm-air plenum, it holds heating ducts that aren't sealed and insulated, or it isn't separated from the heated house by an air barrier under Section 9.25. That last one catches a lot of renovations. Insulate the foundation walls and leave the floor above open, and the crawl space is now inside the envelope.

The trap runs in one direction. A ground cover laid to the unheated article (4 mil, 100 mm laps, loose at the walls) stops complying the day someone sprays the walls. Nobody re-lays poly after the foam is on, so the cheap ground cover becomes the weak point of the most expensive part of the job. Decide which crawl space you are building before the ground cover goes down.

Why the walls usually win

NRCan's renovation guide, Keeping the Heat In, gives both options and favours the walls: insulating the walls means you don't have to insulate and protect every pipe and duct in the space, the walls are usually easier to reach than the underside of a tight floor, and they take less material. Its warning about the floor-only route is what you'd expect, with frozen lines and joist ends sitting on the cold side. Our crawl space insulation post walks through that choice for homeowners.

For a builder the walls have one more advantage. The rim, the walls and the ground cover become one air barrier, continuous with the basement and the floor system above, which is what 9.25.3 is asking for. That only works if the sheet on the ground is part of it.

Our own homepage says it about as bluntly as it can be said: rim joists and crawl spaces are the leakiest part of most Canadian houses, and the geometry is awkward. The ground cover is the biggest surface in that awkward geometry.

Laying a heated crawl space vapor barrier that passes

None of this is hard. It is fiddly, and every failure is at an edge or a hole.

  • Prep the ground first. Rake out rock, roots and construction debris that will puncture the sheet the first time somebody crawls over it. Get water problems fixed before anything goes down. A ground cover over a crawl space that floods every spring just holds the water on top.
  • Use the right roll. CGSB-marked 0.15 mm poly for a heated space. A 4 mil utility sheet is the unheated spec and doesn't become compliant because it's doubled up.
  • 300 mm laps, sealed and weighted. Lay sheets so the laps run with the crawl path, not across it. Use a tape or sealant the maker lists for polyethylene; our vapour barrier tape post covers what holds and what lets go in a cold, damp space.
  • Turn it up and seal the perimeter. The article asks for the edge to be sealed to the foundation wall. The usual detail is to run the sheet up the wall and seal it there, so the wall insulation and the ground cover meet without a gap at the footing.
  • Seal round every post and pipe. Piers, posts, plumbing and the sump all go through the sheet. Each one is a cut in the air barrier, and NRCan's guidance is to fasten the poly around anything it can't go over and seal it.
  • Protect the traffic route. If trades or a homeowner will crawl to a cleanout, a valve or the sump, protect that path so the sheet isn't shredded by the first service call.
  • Never vent a dryer into it. NRCan says it plainly, and it still turns up.

What fails inspection

The failures are almost always the same handful, and most of them come from laying a crawl space vapor barrier to the unheated rule under a space that is now heated.

  • A 4 mil or unmarked sheet in a crawl space whose walls are insulated.
  • Laps under 300 mm, or laps that are weighted but not sealed.
  • The sheet stopping at the footing with no seal to the wall.
  • Open cuts round posts, the sump and the plumbing.
  • Foam on the walls with no plan for the thermal barrier. Closed-cell foam in a crawl space raises the thermal barrier question; our thermal barrier post covers what the code asks for. NRCan's advice is to ask the building inspector whether a particular crawl space needs a covering, and that answer belongs in the quote, not at the final.
  • Old exterior vents left open in a crawl space that's now heated. NRCan says a heated crawl space still needs some ventilation, but generally not vents to the outside, because of summer condensation.

Before the insulation crew arrives

If the walls and rim are getting closed-cell foam, the ground cover and the foam have to be sequenced, because each one has to seal to the other. Sort these out before the truck shows up.

  • Who lays the ground cover, and when. Either the sheet goes down and up the wall first and the foam is sprayed over its turned-up edge, or the walls are foamed first and the sheet is sealed to the foam. Both can work. What doesn't work is each trade assuming the other one is sealing the joint.
  • The old insulation is out. Damp, fallen and rodent-damaged material comes out before anything new goes in. Crawl spaces are one of the places our insulation removal crews work.
  • Water is dealt with. Grading, downspouts and any active leak, fixed and dry.
  • Ducts and lines are where they're staying. Plumbing and HVAC roughed in and sealed, so nobody cuts the finished ground cover or the foam to move a line.
  • Access. A clear way in for the hose and the crew, and the space cleared of stored junk.

On the foam side, we spray Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA closed-cell foam, installed by CAN/ULC-S705.2 certified applicators. It works out to roughly R-6 per inch and acts as insulation, air barrier and vapour retarder in one application, and it bonds to block and poured foundation. In a crawl space it seals out ground moisture, cold and pests, and stops the cold floor above. The spray foam insulation page has the product details.

When the cheaper crawl space is the right one

Not every crawl space has to be brought inside. Where nothing that can freeze runs through it, an unheated, vented crawl space with insulation in the floor above can be the better assembly for the money. The ground cover is then the simpler article: 0.10 mm poly or roll roofing, lapped 100 mm and weighted, with the vents left working. The floor goes on the warm side of the line, and batts between the joists do that job for less than foam. We install them in floors over crawl spaces, supported so they can't droop. See batt insulation.

What doesn't work is the half-and-half version: some foam on the walls, a floor still insulated, vents still open, a 4 mil sheet on the dirt. That's a crawl space that is neither, and it gets the drawbacks of both. Pick one and build it properly. We will tell you honestly when a cheaper product will do the same job.

Cottage crawl spaces around Omemee

Around Omemee and the rest of the Kawartha Lakes, the building our page describes is very often a three-season place now being lived in year-round. Those buildings share a predictable set of problems: an open or vented crawl space, a floor system exposed to the outdoors, and no air barrier anywhere. Converting one is exactly the moment an unheated crawl space becomes a heated one, and the existing ground cover, if there is one, was almost certainly laid to the unheated rule.

That makes the ground cover a line item in the conversion, not something to inherit. Lift or replace what is there, lay the heated spec, seal it to the walls, then insulate. The rim joist and the four-season cottage posts cover the rest of the building.

We price by area, product and access, and a cramped crawl space is a different job from an open new-build attic at the same square footage. We get into the crawl space to see what is actually there, because we would rather look at the building than quote you a number from a brochure. Book a look through the quote request, so nothing is a surprise at inspection.

undefined — questions builders ask

Does a crawl space vapor barrier have to be sealed?

In a heated crawl space, yes. The OBC asks for 300 mm laps that are sealed and weighted down, or a 50 mm concrete skim coat, with the perimeter sealed to the foundation wall. In an unheated crawl space the article asks for 100 mm laps, weighted down, and does not require the laps or the edge to be sealed.

What thickness of poly goes on a crawl space floor in Ontario?

At least 0.10 mm (4 mil) polyethylene for an unheated crawl space, or roll roofing, asphalt or concrete. A heated crawl space needs at least 0.15 mm (6 mil) poly conforming to CAN/CGSB-51.34-M, installed as part of the air barrier system.

Is a crawl space with insulated walls a heated crawl space?

Usually, yes. If the walls are insulated and the floor above isn't separated from the crawl space by an air barrier, the crawl space is inside the envelope and the heated ground cover rules apply. A crawl space carrying heating ducts that aren't sealed and insulated counts as heated too.

Should the vapour barrier go up the crawl space walls?

In a heated crawl space the perimeter has to be sealed to the foundation wall, and running the sheet up the wall and sealing it there is the usual way to do that. It lets the wall insulation and the ground cover meet as one continuous air barrier instead of leaving a gap at the footing.

Do you need a ground cover if the crawl space walls are spray foamed?

Yes. Foam on the walls doesn't cover the ground, and bare earth keeps giving off moisture into a space that is now part of the house. The ground cover and the wall foam have to seal to each other, which is why it pays to agree the order with the foam crew before either goes in.

Should crawl space vents be closed once there's a ground cover?

It depends which crawl space you have built. An unheated crawl space has to stay ventilated, at least 0.1 m² of vent per 50 m² of floor. In a heated one, NRCan advises some ventilation but generally not vents to the outside, because of summer condensation, so the old vents are usually sealed as part of the job.
