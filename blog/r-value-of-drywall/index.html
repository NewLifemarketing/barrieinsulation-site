R Value of Drywall

Almost nothing on its own. Sometimes the last bit the wall needs.

November 27, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

The r value of drywall is about R-0.45 for 1/2-inch board and R-0.57 for 5/8-inch. On its own that is almost nothing. In a wall that shows SB-12 compliance on the effective line, it can be the last part of the number.

Nobody buys drywall for its thermal resistance, and nobody should. But it is a layer in the assembly, and an effective-R calculation counts every layer from the inside air film to the outside one. Here is what the board adds, what moves a wall more than the board ever will, and the two jobs drywall does that matter far more to your inspection than its R.

The r value of drywall, by thickness

Natural Resources Canada publishes the tables used to calculate effective thermal resistance for opaque assemblies. Their continuous-materials table (Table CM-1) gives gypsum board one rate, RSI 0.0063 per millimetre. Thickness is the only thing that changes the result, so regular, Type X and moisture-resistant board of the same thickness count the same.

The still air against the inside face of the wall is worth more than the board it sits on. The poly is worth nothing at all, and the tables leave it out on purpose. That is the scale to keep in mind for everything below.

Counting every layer of an SB-12 wall

The method in those tables is simple. Look up the framed layer, studs and cavity insulation together, in the frame-cavity table for your stud size and spacing. Then add every continuous layer in series: the air films, the sheathing, the cladding and the drywall.

Here is an ordinary 2x6 wall at 16 inches on centre, with an R22 batt, 11 mm OSB, hollow-backed vinyl, sealed poly and 1/2-inch drywall:

Where the r value of drywall lands in the sum

RSI 3.00 is R17.03, the effective line for above-grade walls in SB-12 package A1. The sum lands on it exactly, and the drywall is 0.08 of it. Leave the board out and the same wall adds up to RSI 2.92, which is under the line.

So the r value of drywall is about 3 percent of this wall. Small, but on a wall designed to sit right on the line, every layer the designer counted has to actually be there. The framed layer does the heavy lifting: an R22 batt is worth RSI 2.55 once the studs take their share, which our post on thermal bridging works through.

On a nominal line, the board counts for nothing

SB-12 prints a nominal line beside the effective one, and you only have to meet one of them. Nominal is the insulation alone: the R22 on the batt label. If your drawings went in on the nominal line, the drywall plays no part in compliance and the cavity is the whole answer. Our RSI to R value post covers how to tell which line your permit used. Check the figures against the SB-12 edition on your permit, not against a remembered number.

What moves a wall more than the r value of drywall

If a wall is short on the effective line, the drywall is the wrong place to look. Measured against the same 2x6 wall at RSI 3.00:

  • 5/8-inch board instead of 1/2-inch: RSI 3.02. Two hundredths.
  • R24 batt instead of R22: RSI 3.11. Five times what the thicker board adds.
  • Studs at 24 inches instead of 16: RSI 3.12 with the same R22 batt, because less of the wall is wood.
  • 25 mm of XPS outside the sheathing: RSI 3.88. Continuous insulation moves the number in a way nothing inside the wall can.
  • Vinyl swapped for a furred rainscreen cladding: RSI 2.89. The tables leave out cladding that sits outside a vented air space, so the siding's 0.11 drops out of the sum.

That last one catches people. The cladding changes late, the walls are already insulated, and the wall that met the line on paper no longer does. Nothing in the cavity changed. If the drawings are on the effective line, a cladding change goes back to the designer before it goes to the siding crew.

Choose drywall thickness for fire separation, ceiling spans and sound. Never choose it for R.

The two jobs drywall does that matter more

It sits on the air barrier, and the R value of drywall assumes still air

Every number in those tables assumes the air in and around the assembly is not moving. Warm air leaking through a gap at a top plate or an electrical box carries heat and moisture straight past the insulation, and no R-value accounts for it. Air sealing is where the savings come from, and it is the thing most Barrie-area homes are missing.

On most Part 9 houses, sealed poly behind the board is the air barrier and the drywall covers it. Some designs make the drywall itself the air barrier, gasketed at plates, boxes and openings. Either way, the layer has to be continuous, and the trade that cuts the last hole in it owns the repair. Our post on vapour barrier vs air barrier covers who owns which gap.

It is the thermal barrier over foam

The Ontario Building Code requires spray foam in occupied spaces to be covered by a thermal barrier. That is usually drywall, or an approved coating where drywall is not practical. On a finished basement wall or a foamed rim behind a finished ceiling, the board that was going on anyway does the job and nobody adds a line item.

The problem is the room that never gets boarded: the mechanical room, the storage space under the stairs, the unfinished end of a basement. If foam is going there, decide who covers it before the drywall crew's scope is set. We flag exactly what your space requires as part of the quote, so nothing is a surprise at inspection. Our thermal barrier post goes through the options.

What has to happen before the board goes up

The drywall is the layer that hides every other layer, so the order before it is where walls pass or fail.

Rough-ins finished

Wiring, plumbing and ducts in before the insulation, not pushed through it afterwards. Insulation split around a finished rough-in is the installation the rating assumes. Insulation pushed aside for a late wire is not.

Insulation and poly done, then inspected

The insulation inspection happens with the cavities open. We leave batt work clean and ready for the building inspector and the drywall crew. Boarding before the inspection means opening walls back up.

The compliance line confirmed

Know whether the walls were submitted on the nominal or the effective line. On the effective line, check that the drawings counted what is actually being built: the board thickness, the sheathing and especially the cladding.

Ceiling board matched to the load

A deep loose-fill attic sits on the ceiling board. Board and fastening on wide truss spacing is covered in our R60 insulation post, and it is a question for before the drywall is ordered.

Thermal barrier assigned

Every foamed area listed, and for each one either drywall in the board scope or a coating in someone's scope.

When the cheaper answer is the right one

Most of the money people spend chasing a few tenths of R is spent in the wrong place.

  • Standard 1/2-inch board does the thermal job. Upgrading to 5/8 for R buys two hundredths of an RSI. Buy it where the code, the span or the sound calls for it.
  • On a nominal R22 line, an R22 batt meets it as rated. In clean, standard-width cavities, nothing beats batt insulation on budget. The whole risk is workmanship, which is why we install batts the way the rating assumes.
  • A wall a little short on the effective line usually needs a better cavity fill, not foam. An R24 fill adds more than any board upgrade. In a 2x6 wall our blown-in BIBS reaches R-24 and fills the cavity completely, and the R22 vs R24 question is worked through in our R22 insulation post.
  • Sound is where board thickness earns its price. For a serious media room we will also talk to you about the drywall and the door, because insulation alone never solves a sound problem completely. More in our post on soundproofing between rooms.

We will tell you honestly when a cheaper product will do the same job.

The envelope decisions that matter most are cheapest to get right before the drywall goes on, so it is worth involving us early. Send the wall section with your quote request and we will tell you what the assembly needs, in writing.

R Value of Drywall — questions builders ask

What is the r value of drywall?

About R-0.45 (RSI 0.08) for 1/2-inch board and R-0.57 (RSI 0.10) for 5/8-inch. Natural Resources Canada's effective-R tables give gypsum board RSI 0.0063 per millimetre, so thickness is the only thing that changes it. That is less than the still air film against the inside of the wall.

Does drywall count toward effective R-value?

Yes. An effective-R calculation adds every continuous layer to the framed layer, from the interior air film to the exterior one, and drywall is one of them. In a 2x6 wall at 16 inches on centre with an R22 batt, OSB, vinyl and 1/2-inch drywall, the layers add up to RSI 3.00, and the drywall is 0.08 of that.

Does drywall count toward the code R-value?

Only on the effective line. SB-12 gives a nominal line and an effective line for each component, and you only have to meet one. The nominal line is the insulation alone, so if your walls were submitted on it, the drywall plays no part in compliance and the cavity insulation is the whole answer.

Is 5/8 drywall better insulation than 1/2?

Not in any way that matters. The extra 1/8 inch adds RSI 0.02, about R-0.1. Going from an R22 to an R24 cavity fill adds five times as much. Choose 5/8 board for fire separation, ceiling spans or sound, never for R.

Does foil-backed drywall add R-value?

Not to the board itself. Foil is a conductor and adds no resistance through the material. A reflective face only helps where it faces an open air space, and in a wall cavity full of insulation it does not have one. What the foil does add is vapour resistance, which matters for where it sits in the assembly.

Is drywall a thermal barrier for spray foam?

Yes. The Ontario Building Code requires spray foam in occupied spaces to be covered by a thermal barrier such as drywall, or by an approved coating where drywall is not practical. We flag exactly what your space requires as part of the quote, so nothing is a surprise at inspection.
