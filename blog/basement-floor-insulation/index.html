Basement Floor Insulation

Every inch on the slab comes off the ceiling.

November 24, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

Basement floor insulation over an existing slab is the one part of a basement finish that changes the dimensions of the room. Everything you put on the concrete comes off the ceiling, lifts the bottom step and moves every door, and those three are what decide the build-up long before the R-value does.

So the question on a renovation isn't really which board to buy. It's how much height the basement can give up, what the code still needs from the room once it has given it, and whether the floor is the place the money does the most good. On a lot of jobs it isn't.

What the code asks of a basement slab

Less than most people expect. Under the prescriptive packages in SB-12, Table 3.1.1.2.A for Zone 1, a slab whose whole surface sits more than 600 mm below grade has no insulation requirement. It's listed as not applicable in every package, A1 through A6. A normal full-depth basement floor falls in that row.

The same table does put a number on two other cases. A heated slab, or any slab within 600 mm of grade, needs R-10 nominal (R-11.13 effective), and the edge of a below-grade slab within 600 mm of grade needs R-10 as well. So a walkout's shallow end, a slab-on-grade addition, or a floor getting radiant tubing or cable is where the requirement bites. Check the energy efficiency design summary before assuming the deep-slab row applies. If someone has added heat to the floor, it doesn't.

What the code does ask of any basement floor insulation you choose to install is in Article 9.25.2.3. The insulation goes in with a reasonably uniform insulating value over the whole area, and Sentence (7) says insulation and vapour barrier have to be protected from mechanical damage by a covering such as gypsum board, plywood, particleboard, OSB, waferboard or hardboard. On a floor that means a structural subfloor. Foam board you can walk on is still foam board, and it isn't a finished floor.

That article text is from the 2012 OBC as amended to 2018. The 2024 edition (O.Reg. 163/24) has been in force since January 2025, so check the wording and numbering against the current code before you put a detail on paper.

Where a basement actually loses its heat

The table above isn't an oversight. The ground under the middle of a deep slab stays far warmer through the winter than the outdoor air the upper walls face, so the centre of the floor is the smallest heat loss in the basement. The losses are at the top of the foundation wall, the rim joist and the edge of the slab, which is exactly where the code puts its numbers. SB-12 asks for wall insulation on a basement and nothing under a deep floor for that reason.

The honest pitch for a basement floor is comfort rather than energy. A slab feels cold to bare feet and to a carpet laid straight on it, and a finished floor on a thermal break feels like a floor upstairs. That's worth something to an owner. It is not the same thing as a lower heating bill, and it shouldn't be sold as one.

The basement floor insulation build-up, layer by layer

Over an existing slab the assembly is short: something to keep slab moisture where it is, rigid insulation, and a subfloor that protects it and carries the finish. Every layer has a thickness, and you add them up before anyone buys material.

Check the slab before basement floor insulation goes down

An insulated floor sitting on a damp slab holds the damp under the subfloor, where nobody sees it until the finish lets go. Before the floor is covered:

  • Look for water history. Tide marks, efflorescence, staining at the slab-wall joint, a floor drain that has backed up. Fix the source first. No floor build-up fixes water coming in.
  • Test for moisture. The plastic-sheet test in ASTM D4263 is the standard quick check: tape a square of clear poly to the slab, leave it, and look for moisture under the sheet or darkening of the concrete.
  • Grind or fill the high spots and cracks. Rigid board bridges small dips and rocks on bumps. A floor that rocks squeaks for the life of the finish.
  • Decide what happens at the floor drain and any cleanouts. They now sit below the finished floor and need a way to reach them.

Board, then a subfloor

The usual detail is rigid foam board laid tight across the slab with the joints taped, a plywood or OSB subfloor over it fastened through to the concrete, and the finish floor on top. Read the RSI printed on the board rather than the thickness. That's what the inspector reads. A board whose facer is low-perm is doing a vapour retarder's job on the warm side of the slab, so think before adding a second sheet of poly that traps moisture between two low-perm layers.

The alternative is a panel product with the insulation and subfloor bonded together, which saves a layer of labour. It also saves height, at the cost of much less insulating value. Either is a legitimate way to put basement floor insulation over a slab. Which is right comes down to the next section.

What the floor build-up costs in height

This is where floors get decided. Insulation, subfloor and finish together are routinely 50 mm or more, and all of it comes off the room.

Ceiling height

Table 9.5.3.1 of the OBC puts basement space at 2,100 mm over at least 75% of the basement area, reduced to 1,950 mm under beams and ducts. A finished room down there may also be read as a bedroom or a living space, which have their own rows in the same table. In an older house with a low basement, a 50 mm floor can be the difference between a room that passes and one that doesn't. Measure from the planned finished floor, not from the slab, before anyone frames a ceiling.

The bottom riser

Article 9.8.4.4 requires risers in a flight to be uniform within 5 mm between adjacent treads and 10 mm between the tallest and shortest. Raise the floor at the foot of the stair by 50 mm and the bottom riser is 50 mm shorter than the rest. That fails, and it's a trip hazard on the step people take without looking. Either the stair is reworked to suit the new floor height, or the build-up is planned around it. It has to be one of the two, and it has to be decided before the subfloor goes down.

Doors, the mechanical room and the drain

  • Interior doors get trimmed, or the frames get reset. Work out which before the casing goes on.
  • A walkout door has a fixed sill. A raised floor inside can leave the threshold a step down or flush, which changes how water is kept out at that door.
  • The furnace and water heater usually stay on the slab, so the floor stops at the mechanical room, and that edge needs a finished transition.
  • The floor drain is now in a pocket below the finished floor. Keep it reachable.

Where the floor meets the wall

A warm floor beside a cold wall base is half a job. The basement floor insulation and the wall insulation have to meet, so the slab edge and the bottom of the wall aren't left as a cold strip around the room. That's also where the air barrier has to be continuous: a gap at the bottom plate is a straight path for basement air into the wall assembly.

That junction is where our part of the job usually sits. On basement walls and rim joists we spray Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA closed-cell foam, installed by CAN/ULC-S705.2 certified applicators, roughly R-6 per inch and insulation, air barrier and vapour retarder in one application. Our spray foam insulation page calls the rim joist one of the biggest single air leaks in a Canadian house and one of the easiest to fix permanently. Where the walls are framed, we install batts in basement stud walls detailed so the assembly dries and doesn't trap moisture, on the batt insulation page. The insulating basement walls and vapour barrier basement posts cover those assemblies in detail.

Agree the order with whoever is doing the floor. If the walls are foamed first, the floor board butts to the foam. If the floor goes down first, the wall insulation has to come down to meet it, not stop on the subfloor with a gap behind the bottom plate.

When the cheaper option is the right one

On a deep basement with an unheated slab, the floor is optional under SB-12 and the walls aren't. If the budget only covers one, do the walls and the rim. That's where the heat goes, and it's the part the code asks for.

If the ceiling is already tight, leave the slab bare under the finish, or use a thin panel subfloor for the feel underfoot, and spend the height on the room. A lost centimetre of ceiling stays lost. A cold-feeling floor can be fixed with a rug.

And if the floor is getting heat in it, or it's within 600 mm of grade, the floor is no longer optional and the conversation is the full R-10 one. Our below slab insulation post covers that case. Where a slab is being poured anyway, insulating under it costs no headroom at all. We will tell you honestly when a cheaper product will do the same job, and on a lot of basement floors the cheaper product is the wall.

Basement floor insulation checklist: before the crew arrives

Most of what holds up a basement finish is a decision nobody made early enough. Settle these before the walls, the rim or the floor are insulated:

  • Finished floor height, measured against the ceiling heights in Table 9.5.3.1 for what the room will be called.
  • The stair, reworked or planned around, so the bottom riser stays within tolerance.
  • Water dealt with and the slab tested dry.
  • Heated or not. Radiant in the floor changes the requirement, and it has to match the energy summary.
  • Who owns the floor-to-wall joint, and in what order the trades go in.
  • Plumbing, drains and electrical at their final locations, so nobody cuts the finished wall or floor to move a line.
  • The space cleared for the re-entry period we give you before we start.

Basement finishes in Midland

We work in Midland as part of Simcoe County. A basement finish there goes through the Town of Midland's building department like anywhere else, and the ceiling height and the stair are on the drawings the examiner reads. Settle the floor build-up before those go in, because changing it after the permit means changing the numbers on it.

The rest of the job is the part we look at in person. We get into the basement to see what is actually there, because we would rather look at your house than quote you a number from a brochure. You get a written price for the walls and the rim, and a straight answer on whether the floor is worth doing at all. Book it through the quote request, so nothing is a surprise at inspection.

Basement Floor Insulation — questions builders ask

Is basement floor insulation required in Ontario?

Not for an ordinary deep basement. Under SB-12's prescriptive packages, a slab whose whole surface is more than 600 mm below grade has no insulation requirement. A heated slab, or a slab within 600 mm of grade, needs R-10 nominal, and so does the edge of a below-grade slab within 600 mm of grade.

How much height does basement floor insulation take?

Board, subfloor and finish together are routinely 50 mm or more. Thinner insulated subfloor panels take less height and give much less insulating value. Measure the finished floor against the ceiling heights in OBC Table 9.5.3.1 before you choose.

Does raising a basement floor affect the stairs?

Yes. The OBC holds risers in a flight to 5 mm between adjacent treads and 10 mm between the tallest and shortest. Raising the floor at the foot of the stair shortens the bottom riser by the full build-up, so the stair has to be reworked or the floor planned around it.

Can foam board on a basement floor be left uncovered?

No. OBC Article 9.25.2.3 requires insulation to be protected from mechanical damage by a covering such as plywood, OSB, particleboard or gypsum board. On a floor that means a subfloor over the board.

Is it better to insulate the basement floor or the walls?

Usually the walls and the rim joist. They lose far more heat than the middle of a deep slab, and they're what SB-12 actually requires. Floor insulation over a slab is mainly a comfort upgrade, so do it when there's height to spare.

Should there be poly under foam board on a basement slab?

Test the slab for moisture first. A low-perm foam board already slows vapour from the slab, so adding another sheet of poly can trap moisture between two low-perm layers. Fix any water problem before any floor goes down.
