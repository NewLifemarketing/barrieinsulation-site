Spray Foam and Mice

Foam takes away the bedding. It doesn't lock the door.

November 5, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

Spray foam and mice come up together because of one hope: that a new insulation job will also end the scratching in the ceiling. Foam changes a lot about a mouse problem. It does not end one by itself, and the difference matters before you spend money.

Here is what closed-cell foam actually does, what it doesn't, and the order that keeps you from paying twice.

What foam takes away from a mouse

Loose insulation is a mouse's ideal home. Fibreglass batts and old blown-in material give them runways to travel through, nesting material to shred, and a warm, dark place nobody looks at. A mouse in your attic or your basement header is usually living in the insulation, not just passing over it.

Closed-cell spray foam removes that. It is sprayed as a liquid, expands into every gap and seam it touches, and cures into a rigid layer bonded to the framing. There is no fluffy layer to tunnel through and nothing to pull apart for a nest. Twenty years on, it is still exactly where it was sprayed.

Sealing the envelope also closes a lot of the small gaps a mouse uses to get from outside to inside: around wiring and pipes, at top plates, and along the rim joist where the house sits on the foundation. We install Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA, a 2 lb closed-cell foam, applied under CAN/ULC-S705.2 by certified applicators, and the air sealing is the main reason people buy it. Fewer mouse gaps comes along with it.

What spray foam and mice have in common: both chew

This is the part a brochure leaves out. Foam is not a pest control product, and a determined rodent can chew through it. Cured foam is rigid, but it is not metal.

Public-health guidance on keeping rodents out (the CDC's rodent pages are the usual reference) puts the opening a house mouse needs at about 6 mm, roughly the width of a pencil. It says to close gaps with materials a mouse cannot get through: steel wool for small holes, and hardware cloth, metal sheeting or cement for larger ones. Foam alone is not on that list.

So the honest version of spray foam and mice is this. Foam makes a house a much worse place for a mouse to live, and it closes a lot of the hidden leaks. The hole a mouse is actively using still needs something it cannot chew.

Spray foam and mice already in the attic

If you have an active problem, foam is not the first step. Old insulation that mice have lived in is full of droppings, nesting material and urine. That is a health issue as well as an insulation one, and it is not something to spray over.

Foam needs a clean, sound surface to bond to, so contaminated insulation comes out first. We do that by hose vacuum to a sealed collection unit outside, so the material leaves through a contained route instead of through your living room. With the old material out, the things it was hiding can finally be seen: the rodent entry points, the leaks, the blocked soffits.

Then the order matters. Take the old insulation out after the way in has been closed, not before. Removing it while the entry point is still open just resets the clock, and you end up paying for clean insulation that the next family of mice moves into.

Where mice get into a rural house

Outside Barrie the houses change. In Oro-Medonte and the rest of Simcoe County, a lot of the work is century farmhouses, cottages being converted to four-season use, and properties with a shop, barn or pole building close by. Older rural houses have more ways in than a newer subdivision house, and these are the places to look first:

  • The rim joist and sill. The gap between the top of the foundation and the wood framing is one of the largest single air leaks in a Canadian house. In an older farmhouse it is often uninsulated and open to the outside in spots.
  • Pipe and wire penetrations. On a rural property that means more holes than a city house has: the well line, the septic, a propane line, an outdoor tap, a dryer vent. Each one is a hole in the foundation or wall, often sealed with whatever was handy on the day.
  • Crawl spaces. An open or vented crawl space is a direct route under the floor, and the insulation down there is often damp, fallen or already chewed.
  • The roofline. Gaps at the soffit, the fascia and the gable ends let mice into the attic without ever touching the ground floor.
  • The attached garage. Door corners and the joint where the garage meets the house.

Spray foam in the rim joist bays and the header spaces closes most of the first item for good. An uninsulated rim joist is also one of the biggest, easiest wins in an older rural house, for heat loss as much as for mice. When you heat with propane, oil, electricity or wood, every dollar of heat that leaks out costs more than it would on natural gas. That is the most common reason rural customers call us.

Spray foam and mice: what to do, in order

  1. Find how they are getting in. A pest control company can trap and do exclusion work. We identify the entry points we find while we are in the space, especially during removal, and tell you where they are.
  2. Close the entry points with something a mouse can't chew. Metal or cement at the active holes, before anything else goes in.
  3. Take out what they have lived in. Contaminated insulation comes out by vacuum, and the space is cleaned.
  4. Seal, then insulate. Foam where the air leaks are: the rim joist, header spaces, the crawl space, and the bypasses in the attic floor. Then depth on top, to current Ontario Building Code.

One code point is worth knowing before a basement or crawl space job. Foam in an occupied space has to be covered by an approved thermal barrier, usually drywall or an approved coating. We say in the quote what your space needs, so nothing is a surprise at inspection.

Where the cheaper option is fine

You don't need foam everywhere to get a house that mice don't like. On a big open attic floor, the usual answer is to seal the bypasses, then blow in depth. Blown-in gives most of the gain for a lot less than foam on a large area, and with the old contaminated material out, it starts clean.

The same goes for a batt top-up. If what is up there is dry, clean and uncontaminated, new batts on top can be fine. If it is mouse-damaged, adding more just buries the problem. We will tell you which one you are looking at. If a cheaper product will do the same job in your house, we will tell you that too.

Spray Foam and Mice — questions homeowners ask

Does spray foam keep mice out?

It helps, but it is not pest control. Closed-cell foam removes the runways and nesting material loose insulation gives mice, and sealing the envelope closes many of the small gaps they use. A determined rodent can still chew through it, so active entry points need metal or cement as well.

Will mice chew through spray foam?

They can. Cured closed-cell foam is rigid, but it is not a barrier a mouse cannot get through. Public-health guidance says to close the holes mice are using with steel wool, hardware cloth, metal sheeting or cement, and foam alone is not on that list.

Can you spray foam over insulation mice have been living in?

No. Foam needs a clean, sound surface to bond to, and insulation full of droppings and nesting material is a health problem in its own right. It comes out first, by vacuum to a sealed collection unit outside, once the entry points have been closed.

Where do mice usually get into an older rural house?

The usual places are the rim joist and sill at the top of the foundation, pipe and wire penetrations such as the well line and propane line, open crawl spaces, gaps at the soffits and gable ends, and the corners of an attached garage door.

Do I need spray foam in the whole house to deal with mice?

No. Foam earns its place in the rim joist, header spaces and crawl spaces, where it seals air leaks and closes gaps at the same time. On a large attic floor, sealing the bypasses and blowing in depth is usually the cheaper answer, and we will tell you when that is the case.
