Vapour Barrier Tape

Tape the laps that float. Seal the ones that land.

November 20, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

Vapour barrier tape is the wrong tool for most of the joints it ends up on. The Ontario Building Code gives a poly joint three ways to pass: taped, sealed with a flexible sealant, or lapped at least 100 mm and clamped. On a typical wall, only a handful of joints actually need tape. The rest land on framing, where a bead of acoustical sealant under the lap and the drywall over it make a better seal for less money.

So this isn't about whether poly needs sealing. It does. It's about which joint gets which material, and the places where a roll of tape on its own is what gets the wall flagged.

What the code asks of a vapour barrier tape joint

Poly on the warm side of a framed wall is doing two jobs. As the vapour barrier it answers to OBC 9.25.4. Where it's also the air barrier, which it usually is in a batt wall, it answers to 9.25.3, and that's where the joint rules sit. Sentence 9.25.3.3.(2) accepts a joint in a flexible sheet air barrier that is sealed with a compatible material such as tape or flexible sealant, or lapped at least 100 mm and clamped, for example between framing and drywall.

Read that as a choice, not a hierarchy. Tape isn't the "proper" option and sealant the shortcut. The code names them side by side, and the clamped lap needs neither. "Compatible" is the word that does the work: a vapour barrier tape the maker lists for polyethylene, applied to a surface and at a temperature its data sheet allows. Our 6 mil poly vapour barrier post covers the sheet itself, CAN/CGSB-51.34, and the lap rules in more detail.

Vapour barrier tape or acoustical sealant: which joint gets which

The two materials go on opposite sides of the poly, and that tells you most of what you need to know.

Acoustical sealant goes under the sheet. It's a non-hardening, non-skinning sealant that stays soft and tacky. You run a bead on the framing, lap the poly onto it, and the drywall clamps the lap against it. Because it never cures hard, it moves with the framing as the lumber dries and the house settles.

Tape goes on top of the sheet. It bonds poly to poly, or poly to a flange or a box. It needs a face to stick to and nothing pushing it off.

So the rule on site comes out simple:

  • A lap that lands on a stud, plate or joist gets lapped 100 mm and clamped by the drywall, or a bead of acoustical sealant under it. No tape needed.
  • A lap that floats in the middle of a bay, with nothing behind it for the drywall to clamp against, gets taped.
  • A tear or a staple-pull gets a patch of poly and vapour barrier tape around all four sides.
  • The top plates, bottom plates and rim get sealant under the poly. Not tape over a gap.
  • Boxes, pans and window or door frames get tape or sealant to the flange, depending on the product. Our vapour barrier electrical box post goes through the box detail.

Where vapour barrier tape alone fails

Tape fails in ways that look fine on the day. It sticks when it goes on, the wall gets inspected, and it lets go a season later behind the drywall. These are the joints where that happens.

The top plate, where vapour barrier tape bridges a corner

The wall poly meets the ceiling poly at the top plate. Taping one sheet to the other across that inside corner puts the tape in tension with nothing behind it. Then the ceiling gets loaded, with batts or blown insulation sitting on the poly, and the drywall crew pushes the sheet up as they board. The tape is the first thing to give. That's the leak our batt FAQ describes: poly that is torn, unsealed at the seams or missing at the top plates is a leak path for warm, humid air, which is how condensation and mould start inside a wall.

The detail that holds is a bead of acoustical sealant along the plate, both sheets lapped onto it, and the drywall clamping the lot.

Lumber, dust and cold

Most tapes are made to bond to the poly, not to framing. A sawn plate is rough, dusty and often damp, and tape stuck to it reads as sealed until the first time somebody leans on the sheet. The same goes for a bottom plate that hasn't been swept, or a subfloor still holding the last rain. Tape on poly that's cold or wet with condensation in an unheated building is the other common one. Check the minimum application temperature on the roll, and get some heat into the building before the poly crew starts if it's below that.

Pipes, ducts and bundles of cable

Tape around a round penetration ends up pleated, and every pleat is a channel. A pipe or duct through the poly wants a gasket or a boot taped to the sheet, or a bead of sealant. A cable bundle wants sealant worked into it, not tape wrapped around the outside.

Concrete

Poly carried down to a foundation wall or slab won't hold with tape on unprimed concrete. That's a sealant joint, and our vapour barrier basement post covers how it's detailed below grade.

What gets flagged at the insulation inspection

The building official sees the insulation and vapour barrier once, before it's covered. That visit isn't about tape versus sealant, since the code accepts either. It's about continuity. The common ones:

  • Floating laps left open. A horizontal lap halfway up a 9-foot wall, stapled but not taped.
  • Top plates with no seal, or with tape bridging the corner and already lifting.
  • Partitions that cut the sheet. An interior wall framed tight to an exterior wall or ceiling before the poly went up, with no strip of poly carried through behind it.
  • Tears left by other trades after the poly went up, often around boxes and at the rim.
  • The wrong tape. Masking or duct tape on a lap, which won't still be holding by the time the drywall's up.
  • Open penetrations. Pipes, vents and cable bundles through the poly with nothing sealing them.

None of these are hard to fix with the wall open. Every one of them means pulling drywall once it's closed. Our vapour barrier vs air barrier post explains why a gap in the air barrier costs so much more than a gap in the vapour barrier.

The cheaper answer is usually less tape

We'll tell you honestly when a cheaper product will do the same job, and this is one of those places. A lap that lands on framing and gets drywall over it doesn't need a run of tape along its whole length. The clamped lap is a code joint on its own. Taping it anyway is money and labour on a joint that was already passing.

A cartridge of acoustical sealant at the plates and the rim usually goes further than the tape would have, and it holds better there. Keep the tape for the joints only tape can do: floating laps, patches, flanges and boxes.

Where the assembly needs one layer to be both the air and vapour barrier at a rim joist, a cathedral ceiling or a basement wall, closed-cell spray foam does it without a sheet to seal. We install Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA to CAN/ULC-S705.2, and once it's applied to manufacturer specifications it is the air and vapour barrier. For one rim, that's often the right call. For a whole wall of batts, sealed poly is the cheaper answer and it works. Our rim joist air leaks post covers where foam earns its price.

What to have ready before the crew arrives

  • Rough-in done and inspected: wiring, plumbing and ducts finished in the exterior walls and ceilings, so nobody cuts the poly afterwards.
  • Boxes on exterior walls and ceilings either airtight or fitted with pans.
  • Partition intersections with a strip of poly already carried through, or left open so it can be.
  • Plates swept and dry, and some heat in the building in cold weather.
  • Who supplies the poly, the vapour barrier tape and the acoustical sealant, settled before the day.
  • Who repairs tears made after the poly goes up. The trade that made the hole is the usual answer, and it's worth putting in writing.

On a batt insulation job, the vapour barrier is detailed and sealed as required, and the job is left clean and ready for the building inspector and the drywall crew. Send the drawings with your quote request and we'll price the poly and its sealing with the insulation, so nothing is left for someone else to finish.

The code references here are to the Ontario Building Code, Section 9.25. Check them against the edition your permit was issued under.

Vapour Barrier Tape — questions builders ask

Do you need vapour barrier tape on every poly lap?

No. OBC 9.25.3.3.(2) accepts a joint that is taped, sealed with a flexible sealant, or lapped at least 100 mm and clamped, for example between framing and drywall. A lap that lands on framing and gets drywall over it passes without tape. Tape the laps that float in the middle of a bay, and patch tears.

Is red sheathing tape okay for poly?

Only if its data sheet lists polyethylene vapour barrier. Sheathing tape is made for sheathing joints. The code asks for a compatible material, so use a vapour barrier tape the maker lists for poly, and apply it within the temperature range on the roll.

What is acoustical sealant, and why is it used instead of tape?

It's a non-hardening, non-skinning sealant that stays soft. It goes on the framing under the poly at plates, rim and openings, and the drywall clamps the lap against it. Because it never cures hard, it keeps sealing as the framing dries and moves. Tape bridging the same joint has nothing behind it.

Can you use duct tape on a vapour barrier?

No. Duct tape and masking tape aren't made for polyethylene and lose their grip, usually after the wall is closed. An inspector who sees them on the laps is likely to flag them.

How do you fix a tear in the poly before drywall?

Cut a patch of the same poly that overlaps the tear on every side, and tape all four edges with a vapour barrier tape listed for polyethylene. Seal any penetration that made the tear, too. The insulation and vapour barrier inspection is the last time the wall is open, so tears get found and fixed before it.
