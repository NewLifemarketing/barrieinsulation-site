Commercial Foam Insulation

Same foam. A different code section, a different list of covers, and a busier site.

November 28, 2026 · Barrie Insulation Systems

Commercial foam insulation is the same closed-cell foam that goes into a house. What changes is everything around it: which part of the code governs the building, what is allowed to cover the foam, and how you keep every other trade out of the space while it goes on.

None of that shows on the drawings as "spray foam, closed-cell, 3 in." It shows up at the framing inspection, at occupancy, or the morning the electricians find the building closed. Here is where a commercial job differs from the houses you already know, and the parts worth settling before the crew is booked.

Which part of the code your commercial foam insulation answers to

The first question is not about foam at all. It is whether the building is a Part 9 building or a Part 3 building, because the foam rules sit in different articles and they do not list the same covers.

Part 9 covers smaller buildings: three storeys or less, a building area of 600 m² or less, in residential, business, mercantile and medium- or low-hazard industrial occupancies. A detached shop, a small office or a storage building usually lands here. Anything bigger, or an assembly, care, treatment or high-hazard occupancy, is Part 3. Part 3 then splits again, into buildings permitted to be of combustible construction and buildings required to be of noncombustible construction.

Part 9: Article 9.10.17.10

Foamed plastic that forms part of a wall or ceiling assembly has to be protected from the adjacent space by an interior finish from Subsections 9.29.4 to 9.29.9, by sheet metal, or by a thermal barrier that meets Sentence 3.1.5.15.(2). The sheet-metal option is not open to a Group C (residential) occupancy. Concealed spaces inside attic or roof spaces, crawl spaces, and wall and ceiling assemblies are exempt.

Part 3: Articles 3.1.4.2 and 3.1.5.15

In combustible construction, Article 3.1.4.2 gives the same three routes as Part 9, but the sheet-metal route is closed to Group A, B and C occupancies. In noncombustible construction, Article 3.1.5.15 allows foam with a flame-spread rating of 500 or less only behind a thermal barrier from a shorter list, and sheet metal is not on it. The same article lets foam go above roof decks, outside foundation walls below grade and under slabs-on-ground without that cover.

Energy compliance moves too. Houses show compliance through SB-12. Most non-residential buildings go through SB-10, so the wall and roof values you carry in your head from residential work do not transfer. Take them from the energy designer, not from the last house.

What can cover commercial foam insulation

Every article above ends in the same place: the foam is protected from the room. On a house that is drywall, almost without thinking. On a commercial job the room may be a wash bay, a warehouse with a 20-foot wall, or a steel building where nobody planned to board anything. The cover has to be chosen, and chosen early.

Two details in that table cause most of the failed inspections. The gypsum and the sheet metal both have to be mechanically fastened to a supporting assembly independent of the insulation. Board screwed through the foam into nothing is not a thermal barrier, however well it is taped. And a coating counts only when that coating, on that foam, was tested to S124. A can of paint from the supplier with "fire" in the name is not the same thing.

Check the foam's flame-spread rating on its CCMC listing as well. Part 3 sets a ceiling of 500, and a taller unsprinklered noncombustible building has its own route for exterior walls, tested to CAN/ULC-S101. That one belongs to the designer, not the site.

The cheap cover most shop owners already own

In a Part 9 shop, warehouse or light-industrial building, the steel liner panel can be the thermal barrier. It has to be at least 0.38 mm, with a melting point of at least 650°C, and fastened to the girts or strapping rather than glued to the foam. If the owner was going to line the walls anyway, that is the barrier, and nobody needs to price drywall or a coating for that area. Put it on the drawings as the barrier so the inspector reads it the same way you do.

The same goes for a plywood or OSB lining. Those are interior finishes under Subsections 9.29.6 and 9.29.9, so on a Part 9 or combustible Part 3 building a shop that was getting a sheathed wall for shelving and impact already has its cover. On a noncombustible building neither the liner nor the wood counts, and the choice comes down to board, masonry, concrete or a tested barrier.

A good question to put to any spray contractor is: what thermal barrier does the code require over the foam in my space, and is it in the price? We flag exactly what your space requires as part of the quote, so nothing is a surprise at inspection. Our post on thermal barriers over spray foam covers the residential side of the same question.

Steel, slabs and big open substrates

A commercial substrate is bigger, colder and dirtier than a house wall. Commercial foam insulation does not care what the building is. It cares what it is sprayed onto, and at what temperature.

The installation standard, CAN/ULC-S705.2, is not a product sticker. It requires trained, certified installers, controlled substrate and ambient conditions, documented lift thicknesses and a daily quality record. On a commercial job that record is worth asking for, because it is the only evidence of what went on the wall once the liner covers it.

  • Steel cladding has to be dry and clean. The inside face of a metal building sweats on a cold morning and frosts overnight in shoulder season. Mill oil on new panels does the same damage to adhesion. Foam sprayed onto either lets go.
  • Lifts, not one pass. Each product's data sheet sets a maximum thickness per pass. Closed-cell foam gives off heat as it cures, and a full depth sprayed at once cooks the core. On a 3-inch roof deck that is two or more passes, and more time than the area suggests.
  • Below the slab, the cover question goes away. Foam under a slab-on-ground is one of the places the code lets it go uncovered, and on a large floor it seals around every drain, stub and floor box in one pass. Our below-slab insulation post goes through what has to be in place first.

The product we install is Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA, a 2 lb closed-cell rigid polyurethane foam, and it is the same product on residential and commercial work. What changes is the substrate and the schedule around it.

Scheduling commercial foam insulation around the other trades

On a house, the spray day is a day the house is empty. On a commercial job there may be a dozen trades who expected to be inside that week. Most of the friction on commercial foam insulation jobs is the schedule, not the foam.

What has to be done before the crew arrives

  • Rough-ins in and inspected. Sprinkler mains, conduit, ductwork, hangers and sleeves through the envelope. Anything mounted to the outer skin after the foam goes on means cutting it back out, and every cut is a hole in the air barrier.
  • Hot work finished. Welding, cutting and grinding near the foam area done before the spray, not after it.
  • Girts and strapping in for the cover. If the barrier is a liner or board, its fastening line has to exist before the foam, or the foam ends up proud of the framing it is supposed to sit behind.
  • The thermal barrier assigned. Every foamed area listed, and for each one a cover that sits in somebody's scope.

The spray zone is closed

Before any foam is mixed, everything that should not be sprayed gets masked and ventilation is set up. The crew sprays in supplied-air respirators. Nobody else is in the work area during application or until the manufacturer's stated re-entry period has passed, and that includes the other trades. We tell you the re-entry time for your job before we start, so book the next trade against it rather than against the hour the spraying stops. Our post on how long spray foam takes to cure has more on the timing.

On a building that is partly occupied, phase it. A tenant space running on one side of a demising wall is a ventilation and re-entry question for that whole zone, not only for the bay being sprayed.

After the commercial foam insulation is on

Excess is trimmed flush, coverage is checked, and the work is left ready for the building inspector and the next trade. One thing foam is not: firestopping. A pipe or cable tray through a fire separation needs a listed firestop system, and spraying foam around it does not make one. Keep that in a firestop scope, not the insulation one.

Where commercial foam insulation is the wrong product

Foam is not always the answer on a commercial building, and paying for it everywhere is the most common way the budget goes wrong.

  • Large, clean, standard-width wall cavities. For those, nothing beats batt insulation on budget. The risk is workmanship, which is why we install batts the way the rating assumes and leave them ready for the building inspector and the drywall crew.
  • A flat ceiling with an attic above it. Loose fill on the ceiling is the cheaper way to depth. Seal the bypasses first, then blow it in.
  • Interior partitions for sound. A demising wall between two units wants sound control, not an air and vapour barrier. Batt or blown-in BIBS does that for less.

Foam earns its price where nothing else seals: irregular framing, the inside of a steel skin, a roof deck, a rim or ledger, the slab. On shops, warehouses, barns and pole buildings, continuous foam coverage over irregular framing is often what makes a space practical to heat. Our garage and shop insulation post works through a smaller version of the same building.

If a cheaper product will do the same job in your building, we will tell you. Send the drawings and the occupancy with your quote request, and say which part of the code the permit is under. We come out, look at the building and put it in writing.

Commercial Foam Insulation — questions builders ask

Does commercial foam insulation need a thermal barrier?

Yes, almost everywhere a person can see it. The Ontario Building Code requires foamed plastic in a wall or ceiling assembly to be protected from the adjacent space. On a Part 9 building that is Article 9.10.17.10; on a Part 3 building it is 3.1.4.2 or 3.1.5.15, depending on construction type. Concealed spaces inside wall, ceiling, attic and roof assemblies and crawl spaces are exempt.

Can a steel liner be the thermal barrier over spray foam?

In many commercial buildings, yes. Sheet metal at least 0.38 mm thick with a melting point of at least 650°C, mechanically fastened to the framing independent of the foam, is an accepted barrier on Part 9 buildings other than residential, and on combustible Part 3 buildings other than Group A, B and C occupancies. It is not on the list for noncombustible construction.

Is spray foam allowed in a noncombustible building?

Yes. Article 3.1.5.15 allows foam with a flame-spread rating of 500 or less behind a listed thermal barrier, such as 12.7 mm gypsum board fastened to the framing, masonry, concrete, or a barrier tested to CAN/ULC-S124 Classification B. It also allows foam above roof decks, outside foundation walls below grade and under slabs-on-ground.

Is commercial spray foam different from residential spray foam?

Usually not in the product. Commercial foam insulation from us is Polarfoam 7300-0 SOYA, the same 2 lb closed-cell foam we install on residential work, under CAN/ULC-S705.2 either way. What changes is the code article, the thermal barrier options, the substrate and the schedule.

Can other trades work in the building while spray foam is being applied?

Not in the work area. Occupants and other trades stay out during application and until the manufacturer's stated re-entry period has passed. We give you the re-entry time for your job before we start, so the next trade can be booked against it.

Does spray foam count as firestopping?

No. A penetration through a fire separation needs a listed firestop system. Foam around a pipe or cable tray is insulation and air sealing, and it does not replace the firestop.
